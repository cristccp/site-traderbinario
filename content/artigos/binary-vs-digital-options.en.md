**The difference between binary and digital options depends on the broker.** Each one uses the names its own way. At some, the digital option is a different format, with a strike price and a variable payout. At others, "digital options" is just the name the broker gives to the whole product.

So instead of memorizing names, it is worth understanding the **four things that really change** from one format to the other. That is what I will show you here.

## Same name, *different products*

Two examples from well-known brokers, checked on their official pages:

- **IQ Option:** separates the two formats. The **binary** option has a payout set before you enter and depends only on the price ending above or below. The **digital** option lets you choose the strike price: the payout changes with that choice, and you can sell the position before expiry.
- **Deriv:** calls the whole fixed-payout product **Digital Options**. Inside it are types like Rise/Fall, Higher/Lower (above or below a barrier), Touch/No Touch, Matches/Differs, Even/Odd and Over/Under (these last three about the last digit of the price, on synthetic indices).

In other words, "digital" at IQ Option and "digital" at Deriv are not the same thing. Before trading, read the contract description on the platform you use.

## The 4 things that *really change*

| | Classic "binary" format | "Digital" format with a strike |
|---|---|---|
| **Reference price** | The price at the moment you enter | You choose the level (strike) |
| **Payout** | Set before you enter | Varies with the strike you choose |
| **Exit before expiry** | Usually not | At some brokers, yes, at the current value |
| **Maximum loss** | The trade amount | The trade amount |

### 1. Reference price

In the classic format, the question is: will the price end above or below where I entered? In the strike format, you choose the level. A more distant strike is harder to reach.

### 2. Payout

In the classic format, you know the payout before you click. In the strike format, **the harder the level, the higher the possible payout and the lower the chance of winning**. A high payout on a distant strike is not an opportunity: it is the broker pricing a small chance.

### 3. Early exit

Some platforms let you sell the position before the end. That can reduce a loss, but the sale value is usually lower than the trade amount when the trade is going against you. And it opens room for impulsive decisions in the middle of the trade.

### 4. Risk

In both formats, the maximum loss is the amount you put in. Neither is "safer". What protects the balance is the trade size and the daily stop, not the name of the contract.

## Which one to *choose*?

For beginners, the simpler format usually helps. Fewer decisions in the middle of the trade mean less room for impulse, and your head stays free for what matters:

:::na-pratica
1. **Practice first on the demo account**, in the format you plan to use.
2. **Read the contract rules** on the platform: is the payout fixed or variable? Can you sell early? What is the expiry?
3. **A small, fixed amount per trade**, around 1% of the balance, and a daily stop set beforehand. See how to set it up in [the best money management for binary options](/en/blog/binary-options-money-management).
4. **Be suspicious of very high payouts:** a distant strike pays more because it wins less often.
5. **Log everything** and compare the formats by your numbers, not by emotion.
:::

## *Summary*

- "Binary" and "digital" mean different things at different brokers.
- At IQ Option, the digital option has a strike, a variable payout and early sale. At Deriv, "Digital Options" is the name of the fixed-payout product.
- What really changes: the reference price, the payout, early exit and the decisions you make in the middle of the trade.
- The maximum loss is the trade amount in both formats. Neither is safer.
- To start: a simple format, a demo account and risk management set beforehand.

The name of the contract does not protect the balance. Money management does. Trade with method.
